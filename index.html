SignSync

Learning sign language through visual digital nudges and spatial computing.

ConceptSpatial ComputingBehavioural DesignApple Vision Pro

Context

A conversation that started it

At the Apple Developer Academy, a conversation with a deaf teacher changed my perspective. We had to use an interpreter, and she told me something that stuck: in many situations, her smartphone is her 'best friend.' It made me realize that communication isn't a given.

I also learned that not all deaf people know sign language, often because traditional courses are inaccessible. That gap felt like something technology could actually bridge.

Challenge

The problem with video

Sign language is motor learning. Small variations in hand position can completely change a gesture's meaning. Yet, most learning tools rely on videos. You watch a screen, try to mimic it, and guess if you got it right.

The challenge was: how do you teach a precise physical skill without trapping the user behind a 2D screen or interrupting them with constant corrections?

Process

Nudges and Ghost Hands

My master's thesis explored how visual digital nudges influence decisions. SignSync tests if those same principles can guide motor learning.

Instead of explicit instructions, the experience uses semi-transparent 'ghost hands' in spatial computing. They act as visual nudges—guiding attention and encouraging imitation in real-time, right next to the user's own hands.

Solution

From translation to imitation

Designed for Apple Vision Pro, SignSync shifts learning from translation (watching a screen and copying) to direct spatial imitation. While currently a research concept focused on sign language, the broader question it asks is whether spatial nudges can transform how we acquire any physical skill—from rehabilitation to education.

Product Design · iOS
Product Design · iOSMay 2026

Deal!

Solving the Abilene Paradox through private decisions.

UX DesignUser FlowsNavigation DesignApple HIG

Context

The Abilene Paradox

"I'm fine with whatever." "If everyone else is down..." "Next time, someone else chooses!"

We've all heard these sentences. It's called the Abilene Paradox: a situation where a group agrees on a plan that nobody actually wanted, just to avoid conflict. During the project, my team and I realized we weren't just designing for a target audience—we were the target audience.

Challenge

Honesty without pressure

If someone suggests grabbing a beer, you might say yes just because you think everyone else wants to. Before you know it, the whole group has agreed on something nobody genuinely wanted to do.

The challenge was: how do we design an experience where friends can express what they actually want to do, without social pressure swaying their answers?

Process & Solution

A private decision loop

We solved this by making preferences invisible. Users create activity cards for things they'd genuinely like to do. Friends then privately swipe on these ideas.

The swipe interaction was deliberately chosen to encourage instinctive, honest responses rather than overthinking. A plan only surfaces when there's a match. Because we were handling delicate social dynamics, privacy was paramount—we integrated Sign in with Apple to hide emails and keep interactions completely confidential by default.

My Contributions

Designing the flow

Developed within a multidisciplinary team of six, my contribution focused on UX design, user flows, and navigation patterns following Apple's Human Interface Guidelines.

One of the main design challenges was structuring an intuitive app flow without relying on a traditional tab bar.

Outcome

Deal! launched via TestFlight.

PolyEscape

A puzzle experience shaped through iterative player testing.

ProductUI/UXGame DesignTeam Project

Context

Two extremes, one gap

Many puzzle games fall into two extremes: too simplistic and shallow, or overly complex with a high cognitive load. PolyEscape was built in Unity with Borum Studio to find the sweet spot: a puzzle-exploration game that is easy to learn, but deeply engaging.

Challenge

Players felt lost

Our first prototype featured a strict UI color system to reduce cognitive load. But testing revealed a deeper problem: mechanical clarity wasn't enough. Players felt lost on every level. They didn't know what to do, where to go, or why it mattered.

Process

Iterating on clarity

Each round of testing translated a single insight into a concrete design decision. Four iterations reshaped how the game communicates without explaining.

SnapYap

Transforming photographs into multisensory memories.

ConceptUX DesignInteraction DesignTeam Project

Context

The Pivot

We initially set out to build a color-to-sound app. But a few days in, our team realized the idea lacked emotional longevity—it was a gimmick, not a product. We scrapped it and pivoted to a much simpler mission: creating something that actually replicates how human memory works.

Challenge

Why not just record a video?

Human memory doesn't work like a video recorder; it stores frozen mental images anchored to specific sounds and feelings. Video recordings, on the other hand, often feel performative and cause sensory overload. The challenge was: how do we design a digital memory that feels as authentic and spontaneous as a real one?

Process

The Ritual of Action

SnapYAP transforms standard photographs into multisensory memories by blending static images with personal voice notes.

To save a photo, the user is required to speak. This isn't just a technical requirement—it's a conscious ritual. It forces you to stop and process the moment. By keeping the visual static and letting the audio become the narrator, SnapYAP acts as a private digital diary rather than a performance.

My Contributions

Shaping the core interaction

Working alongside a team of designers and developers, my role focused on UX/UI design. My main focus was shaping the core interaction flow—specifically the mechanic that ties the act of speaking directly to the image development process.

UI & UX
UI & UXNovember 2025

TV Time

Redesigning a familiar experience through Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines.

UX DesignUI DesignFeature DesignApple HIG

Context

Designing what I use every day

I've used TV Time almost every day for years. When you use an app that much, you don't just see its features—you feel its friction. Despite a great community, the interface felt cluttered, and worst of all, it felt sluggish. I decided to sit down and redesign the tool I open every night.

Challenge

Performance through design

How do you fix an app that feels slow without touching the backend code? The challenge was improving the perceived performance and cleaning up the information architecture, without alienating millions of users who already rely on the current layout.

Process

Applying HIG to real friction

I stripped back the visual noise and rebuilt the core flows strictly following Apple's Human Interface Guidelines (HIG). By leveraging native design patterns, standard typography scales, and familiar navigation structures, the interface not only looked cleaner—it felt immediately faster and more responsive.

Feature

ShUSH! Don't EVEN TRY it!

Beyond the visual cleanup, I designed a brand-new spoiler-protection system. Using a native-feeling toggle and a dynamic blurring system, the feature allows users to safely browse discussions and rate episodes without the constant anxiety of a ruined plot twist.

My Contributions

End-to-End Redesign

As a solo passion project, I handled the entire process: auditing the existing UX, restructuring the information architecture, and designing the new UI to strict HIG standards.

ECLISSI

Creating a visual identity inspired by mystery and elegance.

Brand StrategyLogo DesignVisual IdentityFigmaSocial

Context

A premium event space

ECLISSI is a premium event space designed as an elegant, safe alternative to traditional nightclubs. Targeting a 25-40 demographic, the business goal was clear: move away from one-off events and build a loyal community around a high-end, reassuring nightlife experience.

Challenge

Restraint as differentiation

How do you visually communicate 'premium' without feeling elitist? The challenge was balancing mystery with accessibility. The visual identity needed to signal a sophisticated, secure environment, especially for a female audience, while avoiding the clichés of typical club branding.

Process

A system in near-midnight

The palette starts from a deep, almost midnight black, with cold gold as the only accent — not warm color, but color that signals who belongs. A modern serif carries the headlines; a compact sans handles the body copy.

My Contributions

Strategy through execution

I led the brand strategy and visual execution—translating the business goal of a safe, premium nightlife experience into a comprehensive visual identity, logo design, and social media direction.

Brand StrategyLogo DesignVisual IdentitySocial Direction

Solution

A guideline, fully delivered

A complete brand guideline and digital presence. The visual language establishes ECLISSI not just as a location, but as a modern, sophisticated destination with a clear standard of quality.
